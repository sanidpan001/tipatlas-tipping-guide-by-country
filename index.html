The complete guide to tipping etiquette by country

Tipping is one of the few travel skills where doing the right thing in one country can be a genuine insult in another. In the United States, leaving less than 18 percent at a restaurant can feel like short-changing the person who served you. In Japan, leaving any tip at all can create an awkward chase down the street as the waiter tries to return your "forgotten" money. Between those two extremes sits a whole planet of local customs — service charges baked into the bill, cash-only traditions, card terminals with tip buttons, and countries where a few coins on the table speak louder than a percentage ever could. This guide explains tipping etiquette by country in plain language, so you can walk into any restaurant, taxi, hotel, bar or tour in 64 destinations and know exactly what to do.

Why tipping rules change at every border

Tipping exists for different reasons in different places. In North America, it is effectively part of the wage: servers earn a lower base pay and tips make up the difference, which is why the percentages are the highest in the world. In much of continental Europe, a service charge is written into law or into the menu price, so the tip is a small bonus rather than a salary top-up. In Japan, South Korea and China, tipping was never part of the economic bargain at all — good service is considered a matter of professional pride, and offering extra money can imply that the worker needs charity. Understanding the "why" makes the numbers easy to remember: where tips are wages, tip generously; where service is included, tip lightly; where tipping was never customary, do not tip at all.

The second thing to know is the difference between a service charge and a tip. A service charge is money the business keeps (or distributes however it likes); a tip you hand to a person reaches that person. When a bill in Paris says "service compris," the 15 percent is already handled — anything extra is pure kindness. When a bill in Brazil lists "serviço" at 10 percent, that is the tip, and adding another 10 percent means you paid double. TipAtlas marks these cases clearly in every country card so you never double-tip by accident.

Tipping in Japan: the world's most famous no-tip zone

Few search queries are as common as "tipping in Japan," and the answer is refreshingly simple: do not do it. In Japan, exceptional service is the baseline, not something purchased with extra money. Leaving coins or notes on the table can confuse staff, and in traditional inns and restaurants it may even be taken as an insult — as if you believe the workers cannot do their jobs properly without a bribe. Taxi drivers will return overpaid change to the yen. Hotel porters will bow and decline. Tour guides consider their fee complete.

So how do you show gratitude in Japan? The traditional gesture is a small wrapped gift, called omiyage, given to someone who went out of their way for you — your ryokan host, for instance. A sincere thank-you, a deep bow, and a positive review carry more weight than any cash. South Korea follows a similar logic: tipping is not customary, upscale restaurants add a service charge, and offered tips may be politely refused. Mainland China is the same — tipping is not part of the culture and can create awkwardness, although Hong Kong and Macau commonly add 10 percent service charges. TipAtlas flags all three with a prominent "don't tip here" alert, and the bill calculator automatically applies a zero-percent norm so you never calculate a tip you should not pay.

How much to tip in Europe: a region of small, smart tips

"How much to tip in Europe" is a trickier question than it looks, because Europe is not one tipping culture — it is about a dozen of them. The good news is that almost everywhere on the continent, the amounts are modest and the stakes are low. France includes 15 percent service by law, so rounding up or adding 5 to 10 percent for genuinely great service is plenty. Italy adds a coperto cover charge to the bill, which means extra tipping is optional; a euro or two for exceptional service is generous. Spain, Portugal and Greece sit in the 5 to 10 percent band, and locals often simply round up the bill.

Germany and Austria have their own ritual: you tell the server the total including your tip while paying — "Stimmt so" (keep the change) is the classic phrase — and you hand the money directly rather than leaving it on the table. In Scandinavia the expectations fall even further. Sweden and Norway accept 5 to 10 percent for good service, Finland and Denmark are satisfied with simple rounding, and Iceland barely tips at all: service charges are built into prices and nobody expects extra. Eastern Europe is the exception that proves the rule, with Poland, Czechia, Hungary and Croatia clustering around a clean 10 percent. The United Kingdom and Ireland sit at 10 to 12.5 percent, but with a critical habit to build: always check whether a service charge is already printed on the bill, because in London and Dublin it usually is.

North America: where the tip is part of the paycheck

The United States has the highest tipping expectations on Earth, and they keep creeping upward. Eighteen to 20 percent at restaurants is the current norm, 10 to 15 percent for taxis, a dollar or two per drink at bars, and 10 to 20 percent for tour guides. Housekeeping gets two to five dollars per night. Canada mirrors these numbers closely. Mexico is gentler — 10 to 15 percent at restaurants, known as the propina — but with a trap for visitors: the propina is often added to the bill already, so check before tipping twice. The single most useful habit in North America is reading the bill line by line before adding anything.

The Middle East and Africa: cash, small notes, and baksheesh

Across the Middle East, 10 percent is the number to memorize. The United Arab Emirates, Saudi Arabia, Qatar, Israel and Jordan all cluster around it, though Dubai and Doha restaurants frequently add a service charge, so the check-the-bill habit applies here too. In Egypt, tipping shades into baksheesh — small cash payments woven through daily service life — and keeping small-denomination notes on you is a genuine travel skill. Morocco and South Africa sit at 10 to 15 percent, while Kenya and Tanzania add a layer Americans will recognize from safari brochures: tipping guides, drivers and lodge staff 10 to 15 percent is customary, and on a Kilimanjaro trek the crew traditionally shares a pooled tip at the end. Nigeria keeps things modest at 5 to 10 percent.

Asia beyond Japan: service charges and the plus-plus trap

Southeast Asia loves its service charges. Thailand, Indonesia, Malaysia, the Philippines and Sri Lanka routinely add 10 percent at restaurants — and then menus display prices with a cryptic "++", meaning service charge plus tax are both added on top. Singapore is the cleanest case of all: 10 percent service charge plus goods-and-services tax, and no further tipping expected anywhere. India sits at about 10 percent, but city restaurants increasingly add a service charge, so the bill-check habit travels well here too. Vietnam and Cambodia are the region's friendly outliers: tipping is a newer custom, small amounts are warmly appreciated, and a 10 to 15 percent tip for a tour guide is genuinely generous.

Latin America and the Caribbean: read the servicio line

Brazil, Chile and Colombia commonly add 10 percent to the restaurant bill — in Brazil it is labeled "serviço," and it is the tip, full stop. Argentina expects about 10 percent but adds a cubierto cover charge that is not the tip, so the two should not be confused. Peru keeps it simple at 10 percent. Costa Rica is unique: a 10 percent service charge is added by law, literally called the impuesto de servicio, so no extra tip is expected at restaurants. Cuba runs on cash and small discretionary tips, while Jamaica and the Bahamas lean higher at 10 to 15 percent, with the Bahamas using 15 percent as its standard number. Australia and New Zealand close out the map as the relaxed end of the spectrum: a 10 percent restaurant tip in Australia is a generous bonus rather than a duty, and in New Zealand tipping is barely part of the culture at all.

Cash vs card: the detail most guides skip

Knowing the percentage is only half the battle — the other half is getting the money to the right person. In Japan, Germany, Mexico, India, Egypt and much of Southeast Asia, cash is the reliable route: card terminals often lack a tip option, and even where they have one, the money may never reach the individual worker. In Scandinavia and the Netherlands the opposite is true — societies are nearly cashless, and the polite move is to add the tip on the card terminal when paying. France and Italy sit in the cash camp: small coins and notes handed directly are the tradition. Every TipAtlas country card includes cash-vs-card guidance, because a 15 percent tip that disappears into a company's revenue is not really a tip at all.

The five services, decoded

TipAtlas tracks five categories because each has its own logic. Restaurants carry the highest percentages and the most variation, from 20 percent in the US to zero in Japan. Taxis are usually handled by rounding up the fare — a universal gesture that works from Berlin to Bangkok. Hotels are about fixed small amounts: a couple of dollars, euros or pounds per night for housekeeping, and a per-bag tip for the bellhop. Bars range from a dollar a drink in the US to nothing at all across much of Europe and Asia. Tours and guides are the most consistently tipped category worldwide: about 10 percent almost everywhere, rising to 15 or 20 percent for private, full-day, safari or trekking guides. Learn those five patterns and you can improvise gracefully anywhere.

Verify locally: guidance, not gospel

Every norm on this page is baked in from widely published public knowledge, and it is marked as guidance for a reason. Tipping customs drift: tourist districts often expect more than residential neighborhoods, inflation nudges percentages upward over the years, and individual venues set their own policies. Treat TipAtlas as your starting point — then verify locally by asking your hotel concierge, watching what locals do, or simply reading the bill carefully. The two habits that prevent nearly every tipping mistake in the world are checking for a service charge before adding anything, and carrying small-denomination cash in the local currency. Master those, and the percentages take care of themselves.

Frequently asked questions

Is tipping in Japan really rude?+

It can be. In Japan, superb service is the professional standard, not something bought with extra money — staff may chase you to return a "forgotten" tip. Show thanks with a sincere thank-you, a bow, or a small wrapped gift (omiyage) instead of cash.

How much should I tip in Europe?+

Most of continental Europe expects 5–10%: France includes 15% service by law, Italy adds a coperto cover charge, and Germany prefers you state the rounded total while paying. Eastern Europe clusters around 10%, the UK and Ireland around 10–12.5% — always check whether a service charge is already on the bill.

Should I tip on the pre-tax or post-tax bill?+

Etiquette purists tip on the pre-tax subtotal, since tax is not service. In practice both are socially acceptable — the difference on a normal bill is small. TipAtlas's calculator has a toggle so you can see both numbers and choose.

Is it better to tip in cash or by card?+

Cash is the safer choice in most of the world — card-terminal tips do not always reach the individual worker, and many countries' terminals lack a tip option entirely. The exceptions are near-cashless societies like Sweden, Norway and the Netherlands, where adding the tip on the terminal is the norm.

What does "service charge" mean on my bill?+

A service charge is money the business collects — it may or may not reach your server — while a tip you hand to a person reaches that person. If your bill shows "service compris," "serviço," or a 10–15% service line, the tip is already handled and adding more means double-tipping.

Which countries should I never tip in?+

Japan, South Korea and mainland China are the big three where tipping is not customary and can cause confusion or offense. Iceland, Singapore and New Zealand also expect little to nothing, since service charges are built into prices.
